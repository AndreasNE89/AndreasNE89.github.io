Brolly — Privacy Policy

Last updated: 14 November 2026

Brolly has no accounts, no advertising, no analytics, and no trackers. Weather and city-search requests go to a single server of ours; this page explains each of them, and exactly what each one contains. The one exception is the optional one-time upgrade, which is handled by a payment service, ExtensionPay (extensionpay.com). The extension contacts ExtensionPay only after the upgrade's checkout page or its login page has been opened, on this computer or on another one whose key your browser's sync has copied here, and those requests are described under “The optional purchase” below. If neither page has been opened, nothing Brolly stores identifies your install. (In Firefox, the browser itself adds an identifier for the installation to every request; see “The requests it makes”.) If one has, ExtensionPay issues a random key for this install, which is kept in your browser; if you then buy, the email address you paid with is kept there too. Those two are the only identifiers Brolly ever holds, and the email is the only personal information. Both are in the table below.

Brolly is available for Chrome (and other browsers that install from the Chrome Web Store) and for Firefox. This policy covers both. Where the two browsers behave differently, it says so.

What the extension stores, and where

Everything is stored in your browser, using the browser's own extension storage, in two areas: storage.local, which stays on this device, and storage.sync, which your browser may copy to your account, as described at the end of this paragraph. No copy of either is uploaded to us. Two stored items do take part in requests to our server, and each is described where its request is: the coordinates of a city, which are rounded to two decimals before they leave your browser (see “On precision” below), and the publication time of the forecast you already hold, which is sent back exactly as it is stored (see “The requests it makes”). The only stored item Brolly sends to anyone other than us is the payment library's key, which goes to ExtensionPay and only to ExtensionPay, as described under “The optional purchase”. Whether storage.sync leaves this device is decided by your browser's own sync feature, not by Brolly. In Chrome, if Chrome Sync is on, Chrome copies whatever is in storage.sync to your Google account so your other computers receive it; that copy is handled by Google under its own privacy policy. In Firefox, the same happens only if you are signed in to Firefox Sync with a Mozilla account and have ticked Add-ons among the things it syncs; that copy is handled by Mozilla under its own privacy policy. If sync is off, storage.sync stays on this device. Either way, the copy is not made by us and does not reach us.

Nothing Brolly stores in your browser falls outside that table. Uninstalling the extension removes all of it, and no copy of it exists on any server of ours. ExtensionPay keeps its own record of any key it has issued and of any purchase, on its own servers.

On precision. A saved city is held at the coordinates the place list gives for it, to four decimal places, and each stored item is filed under those same coordinates so that one city's data is told apart from another's. That is finer than the two decimals a request carries, and the difference is deliberate: these coordinates identify a town in a public place list rather than a position of yours. Brolly never sends them to us or to any weather service: every request rounds to two decimals first, as described below. If your browser's sync is on for extensions, your browser copies your settings, including these coordinates, to your Google account (Chrome Sync) or your Mozilla account (Firefox Sync), as described above.

What we never ask for

  • Your device location. Brolly does not request the geolocation permission. You type a city name; that is the only location it knows.
  • Your browsing. It does not request access to the pages you visit, your history, your tabs, or your bookmarks, and it runs no code on any website.
  • Your identity. There is no account and no sign-in. If you open the upgrade's checkout or login page, our payment provider issues a random key for this install. If you buy Brolly Pro, it also stores the email address you used at checkout, so the purchase can be restored on another computer. Those are the only identifiers Brolly creates or stores, and both are listed in the table above. If neither page has been opened, here or on a computer your browser syncs Brolly's storage with, Brolly holds no identifier. In Firefox, the browser also adds an identifier of its own for the installation to every request Brolly makes, as described under “The requests it makes”; Brolly does not store it, and our server does not record it.

The requests it makes

To show a forecast, the extension asks our own server for the weather at the coordinates of the city you picked. That request contains:

  • A latitude and longitude, rounded to two decimals — about a one-kilometre grid square. This is deliberately imprecise: it is the centre of the town you chose, not a position, and rounding also lets everyone in the same town share one cached answer.
  • Your IP address, because every internet request necessarily includes one.
  • The publication time of the forecast already on your machine, once there is one — so the server can answer “that one is still current” rather than send it again. It is the weather model's own timestamp, the same for everyone holding that forecast, and it describes the data rather than you.

That is everything Brolly puts in a forecast request. Your browser also adds the standard headers it attaches to every request: its name and version and your operating system (User-Agent), your preferred languages (Accept-Language), and the address of the extension making the request (Origin). What that address contains depends on the browser:

  • In Chrome, and in other browsers that install Brolly from the Chrome Web Store, it is Brolly's extension id, which is the same for every Brolly user.
  • In Firefox, it is moz-extension:// followed by an identifier that Firefox generates at random when Brolly is installed. It differs from one installation to the next, and it stays the same for as long as that installation exists, so every request from one Firefox installation carries the same value.

Brolly does not choose that value and does not store it. Our server uses it only to refuse requests made by ordinary websites rather than by a browser extension, and it does not record it. Brolly adds no identifier of its own to these requests, and no cookie.

We keep no logs of it. The server keeps no record of the requests it answers: not the IP address, not the headers listed above, and not the Origin value. It stores cached forecasts keyed by rounded coordinates — never by IP, and never by user. It sets no cookies. A hundred people in one town are answered from the same cached forecast, because what the server looks up is the rounded coordinate, not who asked. The one thing the server counts is how often its cache was hit or missed since it last restarted: running totals, held in memory, with nothing in them about who asked.

The extension makes four other kinds of request, all to that same server:

  • Official weather warnings — the same rounded coordinates. Where an official warning service can be asked about a point, our server asks it; for most of the world there is none to ask, and the request goes no further than our server.
  • Precipitation for the next couple of hours, where weather radar reaches — again the same rounded coordinates.
  • Geomagnetic activity, for the northern-lights forecast — no coordinates, and no parameters of any kind. What comes back is planetary: one set of figures that is the same everywhere on earth. So this is one fixed address, identical for every user. It is requested only if you turn that activity on and the city you chose is far enough north or south for an aurora to be possible there.
  • A city search — the term you typed, answered from a city list the server already holds. Search terms are not logged either.

Those answers are cached the way forecasts are: under the rounded coordinates, under the search term, or — for the planetary figures — under a single fixed address that carries nothing about you. Never under an IP address, and never under a user.

The optional purchase

The one-time upgrade is sold through ExtensionPay (extensionpay.com), a payment service for browser extensions; the payment itself is taken by Stripe on its own checkout page. These are the only requests Brolly makes to anyone other than our own server, and none of them happens until you start it, on this computer or on one your browser syncs Brolly's storage with:

  • When you click to buy the upgrade, or to restore one you already bought, and this install has no key yet, the extension asks extensionpay.com for one. That request carries nothing else, except that a developer's test copy of the extension says it is one. The key that comes back is random, and is kept in storage.sync as listed in the table. The page you asked for then opens on extensionpay.com with that key in its address.
  • Once a key exists, the extension asks extensionpay.com whether it has been paid for. That request carries the key. It is made about once a day; at most every two minutes during the two hours after you open the checkout or login page, so that a purchase is noticed quickly; when you press Refresh in the popup while nothing on record unlocks the paid tier (no purchase, a refund, or a purchase not confirmed for 30 days); and, after an attempt that failed, again three hours later. The answer is stored as listed in the table. Because the key is kept in storage.sync, if your browser's sync copies it to another computer where Brolly is installed, Brolly there asks the same question on the same schedule. In Firefox, that question is asked only on a computer where you have agreed to the optional data permissions described under “Firefox's data-collection consent”.
  • Without a key, nothing is asked. The payment library answers “not paid” from inside your browser, and no request is sent.

These requests go from your browser straight to ExtensionPay, not through our server, so ExtensionPay receives your IP address and the standard headers your browser adds, as well as the key. ExtensionPay keeps its own record of the keys it issues and of purchases. The checkout and login pages are ExtensionPay's and Stripe's own websites, and what you enter there is handled under their own privacy policies.

Firefox's data-collection consent

Firefox has extensions declare what kinds of data they send, and asks you to agree. Brolly declares the following, and nothing else:

  • Location, required. Firefox asks for this when you install Brolly. It covers the two things described under “The requests it makes”: the rounded coordinates of the city you chose, which every weather request carries, and the city name you type into the search. Brolly cannot show weather without them, which is why this one is required.
  • Personally identifying information, and financial and payment information, optional. Brolly asks Firefox for these only when you click to buy the upgrade or to restore one you already bought, before the checkout or login page opens and before a key is requested; Firefox asks you, unless you have already agreed. They cover what “The optional purchase” describes: the random key ExtensionPay issues for this install, and ExtensionPay's record of whether you have paid, with the email address you paid with if you bought. Your card details are not among them; they never reach Brolly. If you decline, the checkout or login page does not open, no key is requested, and Brolly works as before without the upgrade. Without your agreement on this computer, Brolly does not contact ExtensionPay from it at all: not about a key that Firefox Sync has copied here from another computer, and not about a key made before you switched these permissions off in Firefox's add-ons settings. Switching them off does not delete such a key, but it is no longer checked.

Chrome has no such prompt. In Chrome, the payment requests are made at the same clicks, without it.

Who else is involved

Our server fetches forecasts from the Norwegian Meteorological Institute (MET Norway), passing along only the rounded coordinates. The short-range precipitation series comes from the same institute, requested the same way. So do official warnings, for coordinates in a broad band of northern Europe: that band decides only whether the request is worth making, and MET's warning service issues warnings for Norway, so a point elsewhere in the band is sent to it but has no warning to receive back. Since 31 July 2026 a warning request also carries the language code no when your browser's language is Norwegian, so MET can answer in Norwegian; nothing is sent for any other language. Apart from that code and the rounded coordinate, MET receives nothing about you — not your IP, not anything else — because the request comes from our server, not your browser. Its data is used under CC BY 4.0.

For coordinates in the Americas, and in the US Pacific territories, official warnings come from the US National Weather Service (api.weather.gov). Our server sends it the same rounded coordinates and nothing else. As with MET, your IP address never reaches it, because the request is made by our server rather than by your browser. The service answers for the United States and its territories and refuses points outside them, which is how our server learns there is nothing to ask about a place and stops asking for a day. Its data is a US government work, in the public domain.

Geomagnetic activity, used only for the northern-lights forecast, comes from NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center (services.swpc.noaa.gov). That request carries no coordinates and no parameters at all — it is one fixed address, and what it returns is the same everywhere on earth — so nothing about you or your city reaches it. It is named here for completeness, not because it receives anything.

The city list is built from GeoNames, also used under CC BY 4.0. It is stored on our server; no request is made to GeoNames when you search.

The server runs on Cloudflare Workers. Cloudflare processes the request as our infrastructure provider and may retain transient network-level logs under their own privacy policy.

Notifications

If you leave severe-condition alerts on, notifications are generated on your own machine from the forecast and the official warnings already stored there. Nothing is sent anywhere to produce them, and no push service is involved.

Clicking a notification about an official warning opens that warning's own page on the issuing service's website (MET Norway's or the US National Weather Service's) in a new tab, when the warning came with one. That is an ordinary visit to their website from your browser. Clicking a notification about Brolly's own estimate only dismisses it.

Children

Brolly is not directed at children. It collects no personal information from anyone, of any age, who does not buy the upgrade. The only personal information that can ever appear — a buyer's email, held in the browser by the payment library — comes from an optional purchase a parent or guardian would have to make.
